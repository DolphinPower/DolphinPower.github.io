# 3D Scanner Project
This project aims to develop a 3D scanner by constructing the object from distance sensors. 

In the first two days, we need to connect and calibrate the distance sensors. The sensors used are SHARP GP2Y0A21YK0F, with an effective range of 10-80cm. These communicate and read over a single analog pin instead of I2C and SPI. The operational voltage is between 4.5V and 5.5V. Since the Arduino can output a stable 5V, we have everything we need to begin testing this sensor.
A circuit is constructed with the breadboard and the Arduino. We strip the wires at the end of the distance sensor. They are not solid core, however, which makes plugging in to the breadboard relatively difficult. In the future, it would be better if we can solder the wires to each other to ensure more stable contact.
The 5V pin of the Arduino and the VCC of the sensor is connected to the 5V rail. The GND of the Arduino and the GND of the sensor are connected to the GND rail. The signal pin of the sensor is plugged in to the bread board and another wire on the same copper pad connects to the A0 pin on the Arduino.
With this setup complete, we create a quick sketch on the Arduino IDE to read the sensor. We declare the sensor connected on analog pin A0, then call analogRead on the pin to get the raw value. This analog signal is then passed through the Arduino’s onboard 10-bit analog to digital converter. With a 10 bit ADC the values must be scaled by 2 ^ 10 – 1 = 1024 – 1 = 1023. Dividing the raw reading by 1023 gives the voltage level. Analog sensors output a range of voltages instead of 0 and 1. This range of voltages can then be correlated with distance to achieve readable data.
To calibrate for distance, a graph in the datasheet gives the correlation between voltage and distance as reported by the sensor. 8 points with coordinates are estimated from the datasheet graph and put into a desmos table. A power regression was performed on the points to give a continuous function representing that on the graph. The function can then be applied to the raw reading, with voltage in place of the independent variable, to get the expected distance. 
Finally, some testing. The distance sensor was measured at various points with a ruler to see how consistent the data is. We observe that the sensor we have is off by a factor of about 2, so we simply multiplied all the distances by 2, which gave far better accuracy.
Mr. Raus also pointed out that the second graph on the datasheet is a linearization, showing that the sensor was approximately designed such that the voltage is inversely proportional to the distance in the effective range. In the future, another scale can be established with this function through finding the approximate slope (rise/run) of the linearization. The result from both calibration methods averaged to reduce some random errors.
